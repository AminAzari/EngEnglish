## Course Objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will be able to:

1. **Search scientific literature** using academic databases and effective keyword strategies
2. **Identify and classify scientific sources** (journals, conferences, technical reports, standards, preprints)
3. **Understand publication concepts** including peer review, open access, DOI, and impact metrics
4. **Search IEEE Xplore** and other engineering databases using advanced search techniques
5. **Read and analyze the structure** of scientific and technical papers systematically
6. **Identify and use technical vocabulary** specific to electrical engineering sub-disciplines
7. **Write technically** using appropriate academic register, grammar, and conventions
8. **Write a professional CV** tailored to engineering positions
9. **Write a cover letter** for internships, jobs, or graduate programs
10. **Write professional emails** in academic and industry contexts
11. **Write a short scientific article** following standard paper structure
12. **Summarize scientific papers** accurately and concisely
13. **Paraphrase technical content** without changing meaning
14. **Cite sources correctly** using IEEE citation style
15. **Avoid plagiarism** through proper attribution, paraphrasing, and referencing
16. **Prepare technical presentations** with effective slide design and structure
17. **Present technical topics** clearly and confidently to an engineering audience
18. **Explain graphs, tables, figures, and equations** using appropriate language
19. **Participate in technical discussions** using discourse markers and turn-taking strategies
20. **Answer questions** about technical content in Q&A sessions
21. **Use AI tools and large language models** responsibly and effectively for research, writing, and communication tasks

## Teaching Philosophy

### English THROUGH Electrical Engineering

This course rejects the generic "English for general purposes" approach. Instead, every language skill is taught **through authentic electrical engineering content**. Students do not learn grammar in isolation — they learn it by reading real IEEE papers. They do not practice speaking with generic topics — they present actual circuit designs, signal processing algorithms, or power system analyses.

## 1.1 What is Scientific/Technical English?

### General English vs. Scientific/Technical English

| Feature | General English | Scientific/Technical English |
|---------|----------------|------------------------------|
| **Vocabulary** | Everyday words | Specialized terminology (e.g., *impedance*, *modulation*, *transient response*) |
| **Sentence structure** | Varied, informal OK | Formal, precise, often passive voice |
| **Purpose** | Communication, expression | Reporting results, explaining methods, arguing conclusions |
| **Audience** | General public | Specialists, engineers, researchers |
| **Tone** | Personal, subjective allowed | Impersonal, objective, evidence-based |
| **Examples** | "The signal got weaker" | "The signal-to-noise ratio degraded by 3.2 dB" |
| **Hedging** | Rare | Common: "This suggests...", "It appears that..." |

### Examples from Electrical Engineering

**General English:**
> "We made a better antenna that works really well for 5G phones."

**Scientific/Technical English:**
> "A novel planar antenna design is proposed for sub-6 GHz 5G applications, achieving a measured gain of 7.2 dBi and a bandwidth of 800 MHz (3.3–4.1 GHz), representing a 15% improvement over conventional patch configurations."

**Key differences to notice:**
- Specific numbers and units (7.2 dBi, 800 MHz)
- Passive voice ("is proposed", "representing")
- Technical terminology (planar antenna, sub-6 GHz, patch configurations)
- Hedging and precision (measured gain, not just "gain")
- Comparative language with evidence (15% improvement)

## 1.2 Types of Scientific Sources

### Source Types for Electrical Engineering

#### 1. Peer-Reviewed Journals
- **What**: Articles reviewed by 2–4 expert reviewers before publication
- **Examples**: *IEEE Transactions on Power Electronics*, *IEEE Journal of Solid-State Circuits*
- **Typical length**: 8–15 pages
- **Review time**: 3–12 months
- **Use for**: Establishing theoretical foundations, citing validated results

#### 2. Conference Proceedings
- **What**: Papers presented at academic/professional conferences
- **Examples**: *IEEE INFOCOM*, *IEEE GLOBECOM*, *IEEE ECCE*
- **Typical length**: 4–6 pages
- **Review time**: 1–3 months
- **Use for**: Latest results, emerging topics, work-in-progress

#### 3. Technical Reports
- **What**: Detailed documentation from companies, labs, or standards bodies
- **Examples**: 3GPP Technical Reports, NIST reports, university lab reports
- **Typical length**: 20–200+ pages
- **Use for**: Detailed specifications, implementation guidance

#### 4. Standards Documents
- **What**: Formal specifications defining how systems should work
- **Examples**: IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi), IEEE 1547 (grid interconnection), IEC 61131 (PLCs)
- **Use for**: Design compliance, system specifications, interoperability

#### 5. Technical Magazines
- **What**: Professionally written articles for practitioners (less rigorous review)
- **Examples**: *IEEE Spectrum*, *IEEE Power Electronics Magazine*
- **Use for**: Overview of trends, accessible introductions to topics

#### 6. Preprints
- **What**: Papers posted online BEFORE peer review
- **Examples**: arXiv.org, TechRxiv
- **Use for**: Very latest results (use with caution — not yet validated)

#### 7. Blogs, Websites, Wikipedia
- **What**: Informal or crowd-sourced content
- **Use for**: Initial understanding only — NEVER cite in academic work without verification

## 1.5B AI-Enhanced Research Tools (The New Landscape)

### The Shift in Research Discovery

In addition to traditional academic databases, a new generation of **AI-powered research tools** has emerged. These tools use large language models (LLMs), machine learning, and natural language processing to help researchers discover, understand, and synthesize scientific literature in fundamentally new ways.

> **Important**: AI tools are **supplements** to — not replacements for — traditional databases like IEEE Xplore.

---

### 1. Semantic Scholar

- **URL**: [https://www.semanticscholar.org](https://www.semanticscholar.org)
- **What it does**: AI-powered academic search engine developed by the Allen Institute for AI
- **Key features**:
  - **TLDR summaries**: One-sentence AI-generated summary for each paper
  - **Semantic search**: Understands meaning, not just keyword matching
  - **Research feeds**: Personalized paper recommendations
  - **Citation context**: Shows HOW papers are cited (supporting, contrasting, etc.)
- **Best for**: Exploring a new research area quickly; finding semantically related papers that keyword search might miss
- **EE example**: Searching "improving power converter efficiency with wide-bandgap semiconductors" finds papers even if they use different terminology (e.g., GaN, SiC, WBG)
- **Cost**: Free

### 2. Elicit

- **URL**: [https://elicit.com](https://elicit.com)
- **What it does**: AI research assistant that finds and summarizes papers based on natural language questions
- **Key features**:
  - Ask questions in plain English (e.g., "What methods are used for SOC estimation in lithium-ion batteries?")
  - Extracts key information (methods, sample size, results) across multiple papers
  - Organizes findings into structured tables
  - Identifies research gaps
- **Best for**: Literature review, finding papers that answer a specific question, comparing methodologies across papers
- **EE example**: Asking "What are the advantages of GaN over Si in DC-DC converters?" → generates a table comparing findings from multiple papers
- **Cost**: Free tier available; premium for full features

### 3. Consensus

- **URL**: [https://consensus.app](https://consensus.app)
- **What it does**: Searches across 200+ million papers to find scientific consensus on research questions
- **Key features**:
  - Yes/no meter showing what the research says overall
  - AI-synthesized answers with citations to real papers
  - Filters for study type, sample size, methodology
- **Best for**: Finding out "what does the research say about X?" when you need a quick overview of the evidence
- **EE example**: "Is model predictive control more effective than PID for motor drives?" → shows papers supporting each position
- **Cost**: Free tier available

### 4. Perplexity AI

- **URL**: [https://www.perplexity.ai](https://www.perplexity.ai)
- **What it does**: Conversational AI search that provides answers with inline citations to real sources
- **Key features**:
  - Provides referenced answers (unlike ChatGPT which may fabricate sources)
  - "Academic" focus mode for scholarly sources
  - Follow-up questions for deeper exploration
  - Shows source URLs for verification
- **Best for**: Quick factual questions about technical topics when you need cited sources
- **EE example**: "What is the typical switching frequency for GaN-based power converters?" → answer with citations to specific papers and datasheets
- **Cost**: Free tier available; Pro for advanced features

### 5. Connected Papers

- **URL**: [https://www.connectedpapers.com](https://www.connectedpapers.com)
- **What it does**: Creates visual graphs showing relationships between papers based on co-citation and bibliographic coupling
- **Key features**:
  - Visual similarity graph (papers shown as connected nodes)
  - "Prior works" view (foundational papers)
  - "Derivative works" view (papers that built on it)
  - Shows the intellectual structure of a research area
- **Best for**: Understanding the landscape of a research area; finding related papers you might miss with keyword search; citation network exploration
- **EE example**: Enter one good paper on "MPPT algorithms for solar PV" → see the entire cluster of related research
- **Cost**: Free (limited graphs); premium for unlimited

### 6. Google NotebookLM

- **URL**: [https://notebooklm.google.com](https://notebooklm.google.com)
- **What it does**: AI-powered document analysis tool that answers questions about YOUR uploaded documents
- **Key features**:
  - Upload PDFs of papers and ask questions about them
  - Cross-references information across multiple uploaded documents
  - Generates summaries, outlines, and study guides from your papers
  - All answers grounded in YOUR uploaded sources (reduces hallucination)
- **Best for**: Deep analysis of papers you've already collected; comparing methods across your reading list; preparing literature reviews
- **EE example**: Upload 5 papers on "fault detection in power transformers" → ask "What machine learning methods are used across these papers?" → get a comparative answer with citations to specific papers
- **Cost**: Free (with Google account)

### 7. ChatGPT / Claude / Gemini as Research Assistants

- **What they are**: General-purpose large language models (LLMs) that can assist with research tasks
- **Appropriate uses**:
  - Explaining unfamiliar technical terms or concepts
  - Brainstorming search keywords and synonyms
  - Summarizing or paraphrasing text you provide
  - Helping understand mathematical equations
  - Generating initial outlines for literature reviews
  - Translating technical content between languages
- **Inappropriate uses**:
  - ❌ Asking for paper citations (they may fabricate them!)
  - ❌ Using AI-generated text as your own writing without disclosure
  - ❌ Relying on AI for facts without verification
  - ❌ Submitting AI-generated summaries as your own work

### Comparison Table: Traditional Databases vs. AI Research Tools

| Feature | Traditional Databases (IEEE Xplore, Scopus) | AI Research Tools (Semantic Scholar, Elicit, etc.) |
|---------|----------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **Search method** | Keywords + Boolean operators | Natural language questions |
| **Results** | Exact matches to your query | Semantically related papers (broader discovery) |
| **Reliability** | ★★★★★ — All results are real, verified papers | ★★★★☆ — Real papers, but AI summaries may have errors |
| **Coverage** | Limited to that database's collection | Varies by tool; some cover multiple databases |
| **Citation accuracy** | 100% accurate (direct from database) | Variable — some tools cite accurately, others fabricate |
| **Full-text access** | Yes (with subscription) | Usually links to papers (no direct PDF access) |
| **Structured search** | Excellent (filters, date ranges, publication types) | Limited (less fine-grained control) |
| **Discovery of unexpected papers** | Low (only finds what matches keywords) | High (semantic understanding finds related work) |
| **Speed of overview** | Slow (must read many abstracts) | Fast (AI provides summaries and synthesis) |
| **Reproducibility** | High (same query → same results) | Variable (AI outputs may differ each time) |
| **Best used for** | Systematic, comprehensive literature reviews | Initial exploration, brainstorming, understanding |
| **Required skill** | Boolean logic, keyword strategy | Prompt writing, verification skills |

### 1.5B In-Class Activity: Comparing Traditional and AI Search

### Instructions

**Time**: 25 minutes

You will search for papers on the SAME topic using both IEEE Xplore (traditional) and an AI tool, then compare the results.

**Step 1** (3 min): Choose a topic from the list below:

## 2.1B Using LLMs to Generate Search Keywords

### Why Use AI for Keyword Generation?

Coming up with the right search keywords is one of the hardest parts of academic research. You might not know:
- The correct technical terminology for your topic
- Common abbreviations used in the field
- Alternative terms that different researchers use
- How to combine concepts effectively with Boolean operators

**Large language models (LLMs)** like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini can help brainstorm keywords because they have been trained on vast amounts of academic text and understand technical vocabulary across many fields.

### How to Prompt an LLM for Search Keywords

#### Template Prompt:

> "I'm an electrical engineering student researching **[your topic]**. I need to search for academic papers on IEEE Xplore. Please suggest:
> 1. A list of relevant academic search keywords and synonyms
> 2. Common abbreviations used in this field
> 3. Three Boolean search queries I could use on IEEE Xplore
> 4. Related sub-topics I might want to explore"

#### Example 1: Prompting for Power Electronics Keywords

**Your prompt to ChatGPT/Claude:**
> "I'm researching wireless power transfer for electric vehicles. Suggest academic search keywords and Boolean queries for IEEE Xplore."

**AI response (typical):**

| Category | Suggested Keywords |
|----------|-------------------|
| Core terms | wireless power transfer, inductive charging, contactless power, wireless charging |
| Abbreviations | WPT, IPT (inductive power transfer), EV, BEV |
| Technical terms | magnetic resonance coupling, compensation topology, coil design, power pad |
| Related concepts | misalignment tolerance, foreign object detection, SAE J2954, efficiency optimization |
| Boolean queries | `"wireless power transfer" AND "electric vehicle" AND (efficiency OR optimization)` |
| | `("WPT" OR "inductive charging") AND "EV" AND "coil design"` |
| | `"contactless power" AND "vehicle" AND ("compensation" OR "topology")` |

#### Example 2: Prompting for Signal Processing Keywords

**Your prompt:**
> "I'm researching noise reduction in medical ultrasound images using deep learning. Suggest search terms for IEEE Xplore and relevant conferences."

**AI response (typical):**

| Category | Suggested Keywords |
|----------|-------------------|
| Core terms | ultrasound image denoising, speckle noise reduction, medical image enhancement |
| Deep learning terms | convolutional neural network, U-Net, GAN, autoencoder, DnCNN |
| Abbreviations | CNN, DL, US (ultrasound), SNR, PSNR, SSIM |
| Related | image quality assessment, transfer learning, real-time processing |
| Conferences | IEEE ISBI, IEEE EMBC, IEEE Ultrasonics Symposium |

### Comparing AI-Suggested Keywords vs. Manually Developed Ones

| Aspect | Manually Developed Keywords | AI-Suggested Keywords |
|--------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| **Domain expertise** | Reflects YOUR specific understanding | May include broader/related terms you didn't consider |
| **Precision** | High — based on your exact research question | Variable — may suggest tangential terms |
| **Recall** | May miss synonyms you don't know | Often catches synonyms and abbreviations |
| **Novelty** | Limited to what you already know | Can suggest unexpected connections |
| **Accuracy** | Always correct (you know your field) | Usually correct, but may include outdated or incorrect terms |
| **Effort** | Takes time and domain knowledge | Fast (seconds) |

### ⚠️ Verification: Always Test AI Keywords on Real Databases

AI-suggested keywords must be **verified** before relying on them:

1. **Test on IEEE Xplore**: Does the keyword actually return results?
2. **Check relevance**: Are the results about YOUR topic?
3. **Verify terminology**: Is this the CURRENT term used in the field? (AI may suggest outdated terms)
4. **Check abbreviations**: Does the abbreviation mean what the AI says? (e.g., "ECM" can mean "equivalent circuit model" OR "electronic countermeasures")
5. **Cross-reference with papers**: Do the papers you already know use these terms?

### The Correct Workflow

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ 1. Start with YOUR understanding of the topic   │
│    (even if basic)                              │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Ask an LLM to brainstorm additional          │
│    keywords and synonyms                        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Combine YOUR keywords + AI suggestions       │
│    into a master list                           │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. TEST each keyword on IEEE Xplore             │
│    → Does it return relevant results?           │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 5. REFINE: Keep what works, discard what        │
│    doesn't, add new terms from papers you find  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 6. BUILD your Boolean query from verified terms │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 2.2 Boolean Search Operators

### The Three Operators

| Operator | Function | Effect on Results |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **AND** | Both terms must appear | ↓ Narrows (fewer results) |
| **OR** | Either term can appear | ↑ Broadens (more results) |
| **NOT** | Excludes a term | ↓ Narrows (removes unwanted) |

### Engineering Examples

#### AND — Combining concepts
```
"solar inverter" AND "maximum power point tracking"
→ Only papers that discuss BOTH solar inverters AND MPPT
```

```
"5G" AND "beamforming" AND "energy efficiency"
→ Papers at the intersection of all three topics
```

#### OR — Including synonyms
```
("electric vehicle" OR "EV" OR "battery electric vehicle" OR "BEV")
→ Papers using ANY of these terms (they mean similar things)
```

```
("neural network" OR "deep learning" OR "machine learning" OR "AI")
→ Captures all related AI terms
```

#### NOT — Excluding irrelevant results
```
"power converter" NOT "simulation only"
→ Removes papers that are purely simulation-based
```

```
"antenna" AND "5G" NOT "mmWave"
→ Papers about 5G antennas but NOT millimeter-wave (e.g., sub-6 GHz only)
```

### Combining Operators — Complex Queries

```
("electric vehicle" OR "EV") AND ("battery management" OR "BMS") AND "state of charge"
```

```
("wind turbine" OR "wind energy") AND ("fault detection" OR "condition monitoring") NOT "simulation"
```

**Important**: Use parentheses to group OR terms!

❌ `electric vehicle OR EV AND battery` → Ambiguous!
✅ `("electric vehicle" OR "EV") AND "battery"` → Clear!

## 2.3 Phrase Searching

### Quotation Marks for Exact Phrases

| Search | What You Get |
|--------|-------------|
| `power system stability` (no quotes) | Papers containing *power*, *system*, and *stability* anywhere (maybe unrelated) |
| `"power system stability"` (with quotes) | Papers containing the exact phrase *power system stability* |

### When to Use Phrase Searching

**Always use quotes for:**
- Multi-word technical terms: `"maximum power point tracking"`, `"software defined networking"`
- Specific system names: `"permanent magnet synchronous motor"`, `"voltage source inverter"`
- Method names: `"particle swarm optimization"`, `"model predictive control"`

**Don't use quotes for:**
- Single words: `beamforming`, `MIMO`
- When you want flexibility: searching without quotes for `renewable energy integration` might find "integration of renewable energy" too

## 2.4 Search Refinement Techniques

### Filters Available in IEEE Xplore

| Filter | How to Use |
|--------|-----------|
| **Date range** | Limit to recent papers (e.g., 2020–2025) for current state of the art |
| **Publication type** | Journals only, conferences only, or standards only |
| **Author** | Find all papers by a specific researcher |
| **Affiliation** | Find papers from a specific university or company |
| **Journal/Conference name** | Search within a specific publication |
| **Subject/Topic** | Use IEEE's topic categories |
| **Citation count** | Sort by most cited (indicates influence) |

### Search Strategy: Funnel Approach

```
BROAD SEARCH (many results)
         │
         ▼  Add more keywords
FOCUSED SEARCH (moderate results)
         │
         ▼  Apply date filter (last 5 years)
RECENT WORK (fewer results)
         │
         ▼  Filter by top journals only
HIGH-QUALITY RECENT WORK (manageable results)
         │
         ▼  Read abstracts, select most relevant
YOUR READING LIST (5–15 papers)
```

## 2.5 Citation Tracking

### What is Citation Tracking?

Once you find ONE good paper, you can use it to find more:

| Direction | Meaning | How to Find |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **Cited by** (forward) | Papers that cite YOUR paper (newer work) | Google Scholar "Cited by" link; IEEE Xplore "Citing Documents" |
| **References** (backward) | Papers YOUR paper cites (older foundations) | Reference list at end of paper |
| **Related articles** | Papers on similar topics | IEEE Xplore "Related Articles"; Google Scholar "Related articles" |

### Why Citation Tracking Works

- If Paper A is excellent and cites Papers B, C, D → B, C, D are likely relevant
- If Paper A is cited by Papers X, Y, Z → X, Y, Z build on A's work (newer developments)
- This creates a **citation network** that maps an entire research area

### Example Citation Chain

```
Found: "Energy-Efficient Beamforming for 5G Massive MIMO" (2022)
  │
  ├── References (backward): foundational MIMO papers from 2015-2020
  │
  ├── Cited by (forward): 45 papers that built on this work (2022-2025)
  │
  └── Related: papers on similar topics (other energy-efficiency approaches)
```

## 3.1 Impact Factor (IF)

### What is It?
The Impact Factor measures how often articles in a journal are cited on average.

### How is it Calculated?

```
                    Citations in Year 3 to articles published in Years 1–2
Impact Factor = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                    Total number of articles published in Years 1–2
```

**Example**:
- A journal published 200 articles in 2023 and 2024
- In 2025, those 200 articles received a total of 1,400 citations
- Impact Factor = 1400 / 200 = **7.0**

### What Does a High/Low IF Mean?

| IF Range | Interpretation | EE Examples |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| > 10 | Very high impact | IEEE Journal on Selected Areas in Communications (~16) |
| 5–10 | High impact | IEEE Trans. Power Electronics (~6.7), IEEE Trans. Smart Grid (~8.9) |
| 3–5 | Good/moderate | IEEE Trans. Antennas and Propagation (~4.4), IEEE Access (~3.4) |
| 1–3 | Moderate | Many specialized journals |
| < 1 | Low impact | Very niche or new journals |

### ⚠️ Limitations of Impact Factor

1. **Field-dependent**: Biology journals naturally have higher IFs than engineering journals (biologists cite more)
2. **Favors review articles**: Review papers get cited more than original research
3. **Can be manipulated**: Some journals ask authors to cite their own journal (coercive citation)
4. **2-year window is arbitrary**: Some fields develop slowly; impact appears after 5+ years
5. **Measures the journal, not the paper**: A bad paper in a high-IF journal is still a bad paper!

> **🔑 Key Takeaway**: Impact Factor tells you about the journal's average influence, NOT about any individual paper's quality.

---

## 3.2 The h-index

### What Does it Measure?
The h-index measures a researcher's productivity AND impact combined.

### Definition
> A researcher has an h-index of **h** if they have published **h** papers that have each been cited at least **h** times.

### Example

| Paper # | Citations | Counts toward h-index? |
|---------|-----------|----------------------|
| Paper 1 | 150 | ✅ (≥ 5) |
| Paper 2 | 89 | ✅ (≥ 5) |
| Paper 3 | 45 | ✅ (≥ 5) |
| Paper 4 | 12 | ✅ (≥ 5) |
| Paper 5 | 7 | ✅ (≥ 5) → **h-index = 5** |
| Paper 6 | 3 | ❌ (< 6) |
| Paper 7 | 1 | ❌ (< 7) |

This researcher has h-index = **5** (5 papers with ≥ 5 citations each).

### What is a "Good" h-index?

| Career Stage | Typical h-index |
|-------------|----------------|
| PhD student | 1–5 |
| Early-career researcher | 5–15 |
| Mid-career professor | 15–30 |
| Senior/established professor | 30–60 |
| Field leader | 60+ |

### ⚠️ Limitations of h-index

1. **Favors older researchers**: More years = more papers = higher h-index
2. **Field-dependent**: Some fields cite more than others
3. **Cannot decrease**: Once achieved, h-index never goes down (even if later work is poor)
4. **Ignores context**: Self-citations count; co-authored papers count fully
5. **Doesn't distinguish roles**: First author vs. last author vs. middle author

## 3.3 Journal Quartiles (Q1–Q4)

### What Are Quartiles?
Journals in each field are ranked by Impact Factor and divided into four groups:

| Quartile | Meaning | Rank |
|----------|---------|------|
| **Q1** | Top 25% of journals in the field | Rank 1–25% |
| **Q2** | 25–50% | Rank 26–50% |
| **Q3** | 50–75% | Rank 51–75% |
| **Q4** | Bottom 25% | Rank 76–100% |

### How to Find Quartiles
- **Journal Citation Reports (JCR)** by Clarivate (Web of Science)
- **Scimago Journal Rank (SJR)** — free: [https://www.scimagojr.com](https://www.scimagojr.com)

### EE Journal Quartile Examples

| Journal | Quartile (approx.) | Category |
|---------|-------------------|----------|
| IEEE Trans. Smart Grid | Q1 | Electrical & Electronic Engineering |
| IEEE Trans. Power Electronics | Q1 | Electrical & Electronic Engineering |
| IEEE Access | Q1-Q2 | Engineering (multidisciplinary) |
| IEEE Communications Letters | Q2 | Telecommunications |
| IET Power Electronics | Q2-Q3 | Electrical & Electronic Engineering |

### When Quartiles Matter
- **Selecting where to read**: Prefer Q1/Q2 journals for your literature review
- **Evaluating others' work**: A paper in a Q1 journal has passed higher review standards
- **Planning your own publications**: Aim for the highest quartile journal appropriate for your work

## 3.4 Citation Count

### What Does Citation Count Mean?
The number of times other papers have referenced a specific paper.

### Interpretation

| Citations | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| 0–5 | New paper, or limited impact (normal for recent work) |
| 5–20 | Moderate impact, recognized in the field |
| 20–50 | Significant contribution, well-known in sub-field |
| 50–100 | Highly influential in the sub-field |
| 100–500 | Major contribution, widely cited |
| 500+ | Landmark paper, foundational to the field |

### ⚠️ Important Context

- **Age matters**: A 2024 paper with 5 citations may be more impactful than a 2010 paper with 30
- **Field size matters**: In a niche sub-field, 20 citations might be exceptional
- **Self-citations**: Some authors excessively cite their own work
- **Citation ≠ agreement**: Papers are sometimes cited to be criticized

## 3.5 CRITICAL: High Impact Factor ≠ Good Paper

### Why This Matters

Students often assume:
> "This paper is in IEEE Trans. Power Electronics (IF ≈ 6.7), so it must be excellent."

**This is wrong.** A high-IF journal means the *average* paper is well-cited. Individual papers may still:
- Have methodological flaws
- Present incremental results
- Be poorly written
- Have limited reproducibility
- Be irrelevant to YOUR research question

### How to Evaluate a Paper (Multi-Criteria Approach)

| Criterion | Questions to Ask | Weight |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| **Relevance** | Does it address YOUR research question? | ★★★★★ |
| **Methodology** | Is the method rigorous? Are experiments well-designed? | ★★★★★ |
| **Venue quality** | Is it a reputable, peer-reviewed journal/conference? | ★★★★☆ |
| **Peer review** | Has it been peer-reviewed? (preprints haven't) | ★★★★☆ |
| **Citations** | Is it well-cited by others? (considering age) | ★★★☆☆ |
| **Recency** | Is it recent enough for your purpose? | ★★★☆☆ |
| **Authors** | Are the authors established in this area? | ★★☆☆☆ |
| **Reproducibility** | Could you replicate the work? Are details provided? | ★★★★☆ |
| **Technical quality** | Are equations correct? Are results statistically valid? | ★★★★★ |

## 3.6 Predatory Journals

### What Are They?
"Journals" that charge publication fees but provide little or no legitimate peer review. They exist to make money, not to advance science.

### 🚩 Red Flags of Predatory Journals

1. **Aggressive email solicitation**: "Dear esteemed professor, please submit your valuable work..."
2. **Unrealistically fast review**: "Guaranteed acceptance in 48 hours"
3. **No real peer review**: Paper accepted with no revisions requested
4. **High Article Processing Charges (APC)** with no transparency
5. **Fake or non-existent editorial board**: Members don't know they're listed
6. **No clear scope**: Accepts papers from any field
7. **Mimics legitimate journal names**: "International Journal of Electrical Engineering Research" vs. real journals
8. **No indexing**: Not indexed in Scopus, Web of Science, or IEEE Xplore
9. **Poor website quality**: Grammatical errors, broken links, fake metrics
10. **No retraction policy**: Legitimate journals retract flawed papers; predatory ones don't

### How to Check if a Journal is Legitimate

1. **Is it indexed in Scopus or Web of Science?** → Likely legitimate
2. **Is it on IEEE Xplore?** → Definitely legitimate (for IEEE journals)
3. **Check Beall's List** (or its successors) of potential predatory publishers
4. **Use Think. Check. Submit.** ([https://thinkchecksubmit.org](https://thinkchecksubmit.org))
5. **Ask your supervisor** — when in doubt, always verify

## 3.6B Verifying AI-Generated Information

### Why LLMs Hallucinate

Large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) generate text by predicting the most likely next word based on patterns in their training data. They do NOT:
- Access real databases in real time (unless using specific plugins/tools)
- Verify that their outputs are factually correct
- Distinguish between real and plausible-sounding information
- "Know" whether a citation exists

**This means they can — and frequently do — generate:**
- Paper citations that look real but don't exist
- Author names that are plausible but incorrect
- Statistics and numbers that are fabricated
- Conclusions that no study has actually reached
- Journal names, volume numbers, and DOIs that are wrong

### Why Does This Happen?

| Reason | Explanation | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Pattern completion** | LLMs generate what "looks right" based on training data | Generates a citation in perfect IEEE format — but the paper is fictional |
| **No real-time access** | Most LLMs don't search actual databases | Claims a paper exists because it sounds like papers that should exist |
| **Confident tone** | LLMs present all information with equal confidence | States "According to [1], efficiency reached 99.2%" — but [1] doesn't exist |
| **Training data contamination** | May mix facts from different papers | Attributes the wrong results to the wrong authors |
| **No self-checking** | Cannot verify its own outputs | Does not flag when it's uncertain |

### The Verification Workflow

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│           AI SUGGESTS → YOU VERIFY → THEN USE                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                  │
│  1. AI provides information (citation, statistic, fact)         │
│                         │                                        │
│                         ▼                                        │
│  2. Search for the EXACT citation on IEEE Xplore, Google        │
│     Scholar, or the publisher's website                         │
│                         │                                        │
│                         ▼                                        │
│  3a. FOUND: Open the actual paper and verify the information    │
│      matches what AI claimed                                    │
│  3b. NOT FOUND: The citation is likely fabricated — DISCARD IT  │
│                         │                                        │
│                         ▼                                        │
│  4. If verified, you may cite the paper (because you've read    │
│     it yourself, not because AI told you about it)             │
│                                                                  │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Real Examples of AI Hallucinations in Engineering Contexts

#### Example 1: Fabricated Citation
**Student asked ChatGPT**: "Give me 5 papers on GaN-based DC-DC converters."

**AI responded with**:
> [3] M. Chen, Y. Liu, and K. Ngo, "Design of High-Frequency GaN-Based Isolated DC-DC Converter for Automotive Applications," *IEEE Trans. Power Electronics*, vol. 37, no. 8, pp. 9234–9246, Aug. 2022.

**Verification**: Searching IEEE Xplore for this exact title → **No results found**. The authors exist in the field, but they did NOT write this specific paper. The AI created a plausible-sounding but fictional citation.

#### Example 2: Wrong Statistics
**Student asked Claude**: "What is the typical efficiency of GaN power converters?"

**AI responded**: "Recent studies report efficiencies of 99.3% for GaN-based DC-DC converters at 1 MHz switching frequency [4]."

**Reality**: While some specialized converters achieve >99% efficiency, this specific number with this reference does not exist. The AI generated a "reasonable-sounding" number.

#### Example 3: Mixed-Up Information
**Student asked Gemini**: "What did the paper by Kolar et al. on power density conclude?"

**AI responded** with conclusions that combined findings from THREE different papers by Kolar's group, presenting them as if they came from one paper.

```

╠══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════╣
║                                                                     ║
║  For CITATIONS:                                                     ║
║  □ Search the exact title on IEEE Xplore or Google Scholar          ║
```

### How Different AI Tools Handle Citations

| Tool | Citation Behavior | Reliability | Your Action |
|------|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| **ChatGPT** | Often fabricates citations entirely | ⚠️ LOW — frequently invents papers | NEVER trust; always verify on real databases |
| **Claude** | May fabricate or acknowledge uncertainty | ⚠️ LOW-MODERATE — sometimes admits it's unsure | NEVER trust; always verify |
| **Gemini** | May fabricate, sometimes provides search links | ⚠️ LOW-MODERATE | NEVER trust; verify independently |
| **Perplexity AI** | Provides inline citations with URLs | ★★★★ — cites real sources (verify anyway) | Usually trustworthy; click links to confirm |
| **Semantic Scholar** | Links directly to real papers in its database | ★★★★★ — all papers are real | Highly reliable (it's a real database) |
| **Elicit** | Citations from real academic databases | ★★★★★ — all papers are real | Highly reliable |
| **Consensus** | Citations from real papers with links | ★★★★★ — all papers are real | Highly reliable |
| **Google NotebookLM** | Grounded in YOUR uploaded documents | ★★★★★ — references your own sources | Reliable (but check page numbers) |

### Key Takeaway

> **Use AI tools that are GROUNDED in real databases** (Semantic Scholar, Elicit, Consensus, Perplexity) for finding papers. Use general LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) for **understanding and brainstorming** — but NEVER for citation discovery without independent verification.

## 4.1 Structure of a Research Paper

### Standard Paper Sections (IMRaD + Extensions)

| Section | Purpose | Typical Length | Key Content |
|---------|---------|---------------|-------------|
| **Title** | Identify topic & scope | 8–15 words | Main contribution + context |
| **Abstract** | Summarize entire paper | 150–300 words | Problem, method, results, conclusion |
| **Keywords** | Aid database searching | 3–6 terms | Core concepts of the paper |
| **Introduction** | Motivate the research | 1–2 pages | Problem statement, context, objectives, paper organization |
| **Background / Related Work** | Position within existing research | 1–3 pages | Previous solutions, gaps, how this work differs |
| **Methodology / Proposed Method** | Explain the approach | 2–5 pages | System design, algorithms, mathematical models |
| **Results / Experiments** | Present findings | 2–4 pages | Data, measurements, simulations, comparisons |
| **Discussion** | Interpret results | 1–2 pages | Meaning of results, comparison with others, limitations |
| **Conclusion** | Summarize contributions | 0.5–1 page | Main findings, contributions, future work |
| **References** | Credit sources | 1–3 pages | All cited works (IEEE format: numbered) |

## 4.2 The 12-Step Systematic Reading Process

### Overview

Do NOT read a paper linearly from start to finish (especially on first reading). Use this systematic approach:

| Step | Action | Time (first pass) | Purpose |
|------|--------|-------------------|---------|
| 1 | Read the title | 30 seconds | Identify the topic and scope |
| 2 | Identify keywords | 30 seconds | Understand the core concepts |
| 3 | Read the abstract | 2 minutes | Get the complete summary |
| 4 | Identify the research problem | 2 minutes | What gap or challenge is addressed? |
| 5 | Identify the motivation | 2 minutes | Why does this problem matter? |
| 6 | Identify the methodology | 3 minutes | What approach was used? |
| 7 | Identify the main results | 3 minutes | What was achieved? (numbers!) |
| 8 | Identify the conclusion | 2 minutes | What do the authors claim? |
| 9 | Examine figures and tables | 5 minutes | Visual summary of the work |
| 10 | Identify technical terms | 3 minutes | What vocabulary do you need? |
| 11 | Identify the main contribution | 2 minutes | What is NEW in this paper? |
| 12 | Write your summary | 10 minutes | Consolidate understanding |

**Total first-pass time: ~35 minutes** (much faster than reading every word)

### Detailed Guidance for Each Step

#### Step 1: Read the Title
**Ask**: What is the topic? What is the scope? Is it theoretical or practical?

**Example title**: "A High-Efficiency GaN-Based Totem-Pole Bridgeless PFC Rectifier for Data Center Power Supplies"

**Analysis**:
- Topic: PFC (Power Factor Correction) rectifier design
- Technology: GaN transistors, totem-pole topology
- Application: Data center power supplies
- Emphasis: High efficiency
- Type: Design/implementation paper

#### Step 2: Identify Keywords
**Ask**: What are the 3–6 key concepts? Do I know these terms?

From the title above: `GaN`, `totem-pole`, `bridgeless PFC`, `rectifier`, `data center`, `high efficiency`

#### Step 3: Read the Abstract
**Strategy**: Look for these four elements:
1. **Problem/Context** (first 1–2 sentences)
2. **Method/Approach** (middle sentences)
3. **Key Results** (specific numbers)
4. **Conclusion/Significance** (final sentence)

#### Step 4: Identify the Research Problem
**Look for phrases like**:
- "The main challenge is..."
- "However, existing methods suffer from..."
- "There is a need for..."
- "The limitation of current approaches..."

#### Step 5: Identify the Motivation
**Look for phrases like**:
- "Due to increasing demand for..."
- "With the rapid growth of..."
- "To meet the requirements of..."
- "This is critical because..."

#### Step 6: Identify the Methodology
**Look for**:
- Block diagrams, system architecture figures
- Mathematical equations and derivations
- Algorithm descriptions or pseudocode
- Simulation/experimental setup descriptions

#### Step 7: Identify the Main Results
**Look for**:
- Tables comparing with existing work
- Graphs showing performance metrics
- Specific numbers: "achieved 98.5% efficiency"
- Improvement percentages: "15% better than..."

#### Step 8: Identify the Conclusion
**Read the Conclusion section for**:
- Summary of what was accomplished
- Main contributions listed
- Future work directions

#### Step 9: Examine Figures and Tables
**Figures tell the story**:
- Figure 1 is often the system overview
- Results figures show performance
- Tables often compare with state-of-the-art

#### Step 10: Identify Technical Terms
**Make a list** of terms you don't know or need to learn. Look them up.

#### Step 11: Identify the Main Contribution
**Ask**: What is NEW here? What didn't exist before this paper?
- New algorithm? New design? New analysis? New dataset?

#### Step 12: Write Your Summary
**Combine Steps 4–8 into a coherent paragraph** (see Section 4.4).

## 4.4 Example: Reading a Paper Abstract

### Sample Abstract

> **Title**: "Energy-Efficient Hybrid Precoding for 5G Massive MIMO Systems with Partially-Connected Architecture"
>
> **Abstract**: Massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) is a key enabling technology for fifth-generation (5G) wireless networks. However, the high power consumption of fully-digital precoding architectures limits their practical deployment, particularly in energy-constrained base stations. This paper proposes a novel hybrid analog-digital precoding scheme based on a partially-connected phase shifter network that reduces the number of required RF chains by 75% while maintaining 92% of the spectral efficiency of fully-digital precoding. The proposed algorithm alternately optimizes the analog and digital precoders using manifold optimization and water-filling, respectively. Simulation results over clustered channel models demonstrate that the proposed scheme achieves an energy efficiency of 12.8 bits/Hz/J, which is 3.2× higher than conventional fully-digital precoding and 1.4× higher than existing hybrid schemes. The results confirm that partially-connected architectures offer an attractive trade-off between spectral efficiency and energy consumption for practical 5G massive MIMO deployment.

### Applying the 12-Step Process

| Step | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **1. Title** | Topic: Hybrid precoding for massive MIMO; Focus: Energy efficiency; Architecture: Partially-connected |
| **2. Keywords** | massive MIMO, 5G, hybrid precoding, energy efficiency, partially-connected, phase shifter |
| **3. Abstract** | Complete summary available (see above) |
| **4. Problem** | High power consumption of fully-digital precoding limits practical deployment |
| **5. Motivation** | 5G base stations are energy-constrained; massive MIMO needs practical solutions |
| **6. Method** | Hybrid analog-digital precoding with partially-connected phase shifter network; manifold optimization + water-filling |
| **7. Results** | 75% RF chain reduction; 92% spectral efficiency maintained; 12.8 bits/Hz/J energy efficiency; 3.2× better than fully-digital; 1.4× better than existing hybrid |
| **8. Conclusion** | Partially-connected architecture is an attractive trade-off for practical 5G deployment |

## 4.6B Using AI to Assist Paper Comprehension

### Why Use AI When Reading Papers?

Scientific papers in electrical engineering often contain:
- Unfamiliar technical terminology from adjacent fields
- Complex mathematical derivations
- Dense methodology descriptions
- Implicit assumptions that experts understand but students may not

AI tools can help you **understand** these papers faster — but they cannot replace the process of actually reading and engaging with the paper yourself.

### ✅ Appropriate Uses of AI for Paper Comprehension

| Use Case | Example Prompt | Why It's OK |
|----------|---------------|-------------|
| **Explain unfamiliar terms** | "What is a forgetting-factor recursive least squares algorithm, explained simply?" | You're learning vocabulary — same as using a dictionary |
| **Summarize a section** | "Explain the key idea of this paragraph in simpler terms: [paste paragraph]" | Helps initial understanding; you still read the original |
| **Explain equations** | "What does this equation represent physically? [paste equation]" | Mathematical literacy aid |
| **Clarify methodology** | "What is the difference between EKF and UKF in state estimation?" | Background knowledge building |
| **Translate key sentences** | "Translate this technical sentence to Persian: [paste]" | Language support |
| **Identify key contributions** | "Based on this abstract, what are the 3 main contributions? [paste abstract]" | Helps focus your reading |

### ❌ Inappropriate Uses of AI for Paper Comprehension

| Misuse | Why It's Wrong | Consequence |
|--------|----------------|-------------|
| **Using AI summary AS your understanding** without reading the paper | You haven't engaged with the actual content | Shallow understanding; can't answer questions about it |
| **Asking AI "what does this paper say?" without reading it yourself** | The AI may misinterpret or oversimplify | Wrong conclusions; academic dishonesty |
| **Submitting AI-generated summaries as your own work** | Plagiarism | Academic misconduct; potential course failure |
| **Trusting AI interpretation of results** without checking the paper | AI may misread tables, figures, or context | Incorrect information in YOUR work |
| **Skipping difficult sections** because AI "explained" them | You never develop the skill to read technical papers | Long-term skill deficit |

### Sample Prompts for Paper Comprehension

#### When you encounter an unfamiliar concept:
> "I'm reading a paper about battery SOC estimation. The paper mentions 'forgetting-factor recursive least squares (FFRLS).' Explain what this algorithm does and why it would be used for parameter identification in battery models. Explain at an undergraduate EE level."

#### When you struggle with an equation:
> "This equation appears in a paper on power converters:
> P_loss = I²_rms × R_ds(on) + 0.5 × V_ds × I_d × (t_r + t_f) × f_sw
> Explain what each term represents physically and what this equation calculates."

#### When you need context for a methodology:
> "A paper uses 'manifold optimization' to design beamforming vectors for massive MIMO. I understand linear optimization, but what makes manifold optimization different, and why would it be used here? Keep the explanation suitable for a senior EE undergraduate."

#### When you want to understand a comparison:
> "A paper compares their proposed method against 'standard EKF' and 'UKF' baselines. What are the fundamental differences between Extended Kalman Filter and Unscented Kalman Filter for nonlinear state estimation?"
