## 13.4 Guidelines for Audience Participation

### Asking Questions
Good academic questions demonstrate engagement. Use these frameworks:

| Question Type | Example Starter | EE Example |
|---------------|----------------|------------|
| Clarification | "Could you explain what you mean by…?" | "Could you explain what you mean by 'soft switching' in this converter topology?" |
| Extension | "Have you considered how this applies to…?" | "Have you considered how this MPPT algorithm performs under partial shading conditions?" |
| Comparison | "How does this compare to…?" | "How does GaN compare to SiC in terms of thermal management?" |
| Methodology | "Why did the authors choose…?" | "Why did the authors choose a CNN over an RNN for fault classification?" |
| Implication | "What would this mean for…?" | "What would widespread adoption of this technology mean for grid stability?" |

### Giving Constructive Feedback
- Start with something positive ("I found your explanation of… very clear")
- Be specific, not vague ("The diagram on slide 4 helped me understand the circuit topology")
- Frame suggestions constructively ("It might help to slow down during the mathematical derivations")
- Focus on communication, not just content

## 13.5 Tips for Final Preparation

### One Week Before
1. **Rehearse aloud** at least 3 times with a timer
2. **Record yourself** and review for filler words ("um," "uh," "so basically")
3. **Simplify slides** — aim for max 6 lines of text per slide; use diagrams
4. **Prepare for Q&A** — anticipate 5 likely questions and draft answers
5. **Check pronunciation** of technical terms (e.g., "impedance" /ɪmˈpiːdəns/, "capacitance" /kəˈpæsɪtəns/)

### Presentation Day
- Speak to the **audience**, not to your slides
- Use the **signposting language** practised in Weeks 4–5:
  - "I'd like to begin by outlining…"
  - "Moving on to the experimental results…"
  - "To summarise the key findings…"
- Maintain a pace of **120–140 words per minute**
- Pause after key points to let information register
- If you don't understand a question, say: "Could you rephrase that?" or "Do you mean…?"

## 13.6 AI Literacy Assessment Component

### Purpose
Students demonstrate practical competence in using AI tools responsibly and effectively for academic and research tasks. This assessment evaluates critical thinking about AI outputs, not just the ability to use the tools.

### Assessment Format
- **Duration:** 15 minutes
- **Type:** Individual practical task (in-class, supervised)
- **When:** During Week 13 presentation session (non-presenting students complete this task)

### Skills Assessed

Students must demonstrate they can:

| Skill | Task Description |
|-------|-----------------|
| **a) Use AI to find relevant papers AND verify the results** | Given a research topic, use an AI tool to identify 3 relevant papers, then verify each paper actually exists using IEEE Xplore or Google Scholar |
| **b) Write an effective prompt for a research task** | Write a prompt that would help an AI tool explain a specific EE concept at B2–C1 level, then evaluate the quality of the output |
| **c) Identify hallucinations or errors in AI output** | Given an AI-generated paragraph about an EE topic, identify factual errors, fabricated references, or unsupported claims |
| **d) Know when to use vs. not use AI tools** | Given 5 academic scenarios, classify each as "appropriate AI use," "inappropriate AI use," or "requires human judgement" and justify the classification |

### AI Literacy Scoring Rubric (20 points total)

| Criterion | 5 (Excellent) | 4 (Good) | 3 (Adequate) | 2 (Below Average) | 1 (Poor) | Points |
|-----------|---------------|----------|--------------|-------------------|----------|:------:|
| **Paper Verification** | All 3 papers verified correctly; identifies any fabricated references immediately | 2–3 papers verified; recognises most issues | Attempts verification but misses fabricated results | Minimal verification effort; accepts AI output at face value | No verification attempted | /5 |
| **Prompt Engineering** | Clear, specific, well-structured prompt; produces highly relevant output; iterates effectively | Good prompt with minor improvements needed; adequate output | Basic prompt; output partially relevant; limited iteration | Vague or poorly constructed prompt; irrelevant output | No meaningful prompt; copies generic examples | /5 |
| **Error Identification** | Identifies all errors/hallucinations; explains why each is problematic; suggests corrections | Identifies most errors; provides reasonable explanations | Identifies some errors but misses others; limited explanation | Identifies 1 error at most; cannot explain issues | Fails to identify any errors; accepts all content as accurate | /5 |
| **Appropriate Use Judgement** | All 5 scenarios classified correctly with clear, well-reasoned justifications | 4 scenarios correct with adequate justifications | 3 scenarios correct; justifications present but weak | 1–2 scenarios correct; poor reasoning | No scenarios classified correctly; no understanding of ethical boundaries | /5 |

## 14.2 Writing a Short Technical Article (500–800 words)

### Purpose
Students write a concise technical article based on their presentation topic, demonstrating the ability to communicate complex EE concepts in clear, structured written English suitable for a professional or academic audience.

### Article Structure

| Section | Word Count | Content |
|---------|:----------:|---------|
| **Title** | — | Concise, informative (max 12 words) |
| **Introduction** | 100–150 | Context, problem statement, purpose of article |
| **Background/Theory** | 100–150 | Key concepts, relevant technical principles |
| **Main Discussion** | 200–300 | Analysis of the topic, key findings from the literature, comparison of approaches |
| **Conclusion** | 80–120 | Summary of key points, future outlook |
| **References** | — | Minimum 3 sources in IEEE format |

### Example Titles (EE Topics)
- "Wide-Bandgap Semiconductors: The Future of Power Electronics"
- "How 5G Beamforming Overcomes Millimetre-Wave Propagation Challenges"
- "Machine Learning Approaches to Predictive Maintenance in Power Systems"
- "The Role of Battery Management Systems in Electric Vehicle Safety"

### Writing Requirements
1. **Register:** Formal academic/technical English
2. **Voice:** Primarily active voice; passive where appropriate for objectivity
3. **Tense:** Present simple for established facts; past simple for specific studies
4. **Citations:** Minimum 3 references in IEEE format [1], [2], [3]
5. **Figures:** At least 1 diagram, circuit schematic, or data visualisation (with caption)
6. **Terminology:** Consistent use of technical vocabulary; define acronyms on first use
7. **Formatting:** 12pt font, 1.5 spacing, section headings, numbered references

### Grading Criteria (20 points total)

| Criterion | Points | Description |
|-----------|:------:|-------------|
| Structure & Organisation | /4 | Clear sections, logical flow, appropriate length |
| Technical Content | /5 | Accuracy, depth, relevance to EE |
| Language Quality | /4 | Grammar, vocabulary, register, readability |
| Citations & References | /4 | Proper IEEE format, minimum 3 sources, in-text citations |
| Formatting & Visuals | /3 | Professional layout, at least 1 figure with caption |

## 14.7 Reflection on AI Tools in Academic Work

### Purpose
Students reflect critically on their use of AI tools throughout the course, consolidating their understanding of responsible AI use in academic and professional contexts.

### Reflection Writing Task (200 words)

Students write a **200-word reflection** addressing the following points:

1. **How you used AI:** Which tools did you use during this course? For what specific tasks?
2. **What worked:** In which situations did AI tools genuinely help your learning or productivity?
3. **What didn't work:** When did AI tools fail you, produce errors, or lead you in the wrong direction?
4. **Ethical decisions:** What choices did you make about when to use or not use AI? How did you ensure academic integrity?

### Reflection Prompts
- "Did using AI tools change how you approach research? In what way?"
- "Was there a moment when you realised AI output was wrong? How did you discover the error?"
- "How do you decide what counts as 'your own work' when AI assists in the process?"
- "What would you do differently regarding AI use if you took this course again?"

### Group Discussion: The Future of AI in Engineering (20 minutes)

**Discussion Question:** *"How will AI tools change engineering research and communication in the next 5 years?"*

Discussion framework:

| Aspect | Consider... |
|--------|-------------|
| **Research** | How will AI change how engineers find, read, and synthesise papers? |
| **Writing** | Will AI replace technical writing skills, or make them more important? |
| **Communication** | How might AI affect presentations, collaboration, and peer review? |
| **Ethics** | What new ethical challenges will engineers face with AI tools? |
| **Skills** | Which skills will become more/less valuable as AI tools improve? |

### Discussion Guidelines
- Each student contributes at least one point
- Support opinions with examples from your own experience this semester
- Consider both optimistic and cautious perspectives
- Think about what this means for your career in electrical engineering

### Course Wrap-up: Lifelong Learning with Evolving AI Tools

Key takeaways for students:

- ✅ AI tools will continue to evolve — the specific tools matter less than your ability to evaluate and use them critically
- ✅ Technical communication skills become **more** important with AI, not less — you must verify, edit, and improve AI output
- ✅ Disclosure and transparency are professional obligations, not just academic requirements
- ✅ Your value as an engineer lies in judgement, creativity, and domain expertise — not in tasks AI can automate
- ✅ Develop a personal framework for responsible AI use and update it as technology changes
- ✅ Stay current: follow IEEE, ACM, and your institution's evolving policies on AI use
